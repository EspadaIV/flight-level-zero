## What Is It?

The Cessna 172 Skyhawk is a four-seat, single-engine light airplane. It has a high wing, fixed tricycle landing gear, and a piston engine that turns a propeller.

The pilot and front-seat passenger sit beside each other. Two additional seats and a baggage compartment are located behind them.

The Cessna 172 first flew in 1955. Many versions have been produced since then, so the engine, instruments, performance, and equipment can differ depending on the aircraft’s age and model.

## What Was It Designed to Do?

The Cessna 172 was designed as a practical, general-purpose airplane. It is commonly used for:

- Primary flight training
- Personal transportation
- Passenger flights
- Aircraft rental
- Sightseeing
- Aerial observation and photography
- Patrol and survey work
- Carrying baggage or small amounts of equipment

It is not primarily a cargo airplane, but it can carry baggage and light supplies within its weight-and-balance limits.

Some privately owned Cessna 172s may be approved for missions with the United States Coast Guard Auxiliary. These aircraft can support patrols, search-and-rescue operations, environmental monitoring, and observation missions. They are not part of the Coast Guard’s standard active-duty aircraft fleet.

## Major Features

- Four seats
- One piston engine
- Two-blade propeller
- Wings positioned above the cabin
- Fixed tricycle landing gear
- A nose wheel and two main wheels
- Control yokes for the pilot and copilot
- Large windows
- Wing-mounted fuel tanks
- Conventional tail with a rudder and elevator
- Electrically operated wing flaps on current models
- Stable and predictable handling
- Either traditional round instruments or a modern glass cockpit, depending on the model

## How It Flies

The Cessna 172 is known for stable and predictable handling. It generally responds smoothly rather than abruptly when the pilot moves the controls.

Its high wing provides good downward visibility, which is useful during sightseeing, training, and observation flights. The high wing also allows fuel to flow downward from the wing tanks as part of the fuel system.

The airplane’s relatively low landing speed, tricycle landing gear, and forgiving stall behavior help make it suitable for student pilots.

That does not mean the aircraft flies itself or cannot be mishandled. Pilots must still manage airspeed, altitude, weather, fuel, weight, and aircraft configuration correctly.

## Systems Worth Knowing

### Engine

The current Skyhawk uses a fuel-injected, four-cylinder Lycoming IO-360-L2A engine producing 180 horsepower.

The cylinders are arranged horizontally on opposite sides of the crankcase. This is called a **horizontally opposed engine**. Air flowing around the engine helps remove heat.

Older Cessna 172 models used several different Continental and Lycoming engines.

### Propeller

The current Skyhawk uses a two-blade, fixed-pitch propeller.

The angle of a fixed-pitch propeller’s blades cannot be changed by the pilot during flight. Its design represents a compromise between takeoff, climbing, and cruising performance.

### Fuel System

Fuel is stored in tanks inside the wings. Because the wings are above the engine, gravity helps move fuel toward the engine.

The exact fuel system depends on the aircraft model. Fuel-injected versions also use fuel pumps to maintain the pressure required by the engine.

### Flight Controls

The pilot uses a control yoke to move the ailerons and elevator.

- Turning the yoke moves the ailerons and rolls the airplane.
- Pushing or pulling the yoke moves the elevator and changes the airplane’s pitch.
- Rudder pedals move the rudder and control yaw.
- The rudder pedals also steer the nose wheel while the airplane is moving slowly on the ground.

### Wing Flaps

The flaps are movable surfaces on the rear portion of the wings.

Extending them changes the shape of the wing, increasing lift and drag. Pilots use flaps to help the airplane fly more slowly during takeoff and landing.

### Pitot-Static System

The pitot-static system uses air pressure to operate three important instruments:

- Airspeed indicator
- Altimeter
- Vertical speed indicator

The pitot tube measures air pressure caused by the airplane’s forward movement. Static ports measure the surrounding atmospheric pressure.

### Gyroscopic Instruments

Traditional aircraft panels contain spinning gyroscopes that help show the airplane’s attitude, heading, and rate of turn.

Different aircraft have powered these gyroscopes in different ways.

#### Venturi system

Some early light aircraft used an externally mounted **venturi tube**. It looked somewhat like a small horn.

As air passed through the narrow portion of the venturi, its pressure decreased. This created suction that could spin the gyroscopes inside certain flight instruments.

The venturi did not produce an audible horn sound.

#### Vacuum-pump system

Many later airplanes used an engine-driven vacuum pump instead of an external venturi. The pump created suction for instruments such as the attitude indicator and heading indicator.

A failed pump, broken hose, clogged filter, or leaking instrument could cause the vacuum-powered instruments to become inaccurate.

#### Electronic glass cockpit

Current-production Skyhawks use Garmin G1000 NXi electronic avionics. Flight information appears on large digital screens instead of relying entirely on separate mechanical gyroscopic instruments.

The electronic instruments are powered by the aircraft’s electrical system, not by vacuum.

## Why It Matters

The Cessna 172 is one of the most important training aircraft in aviation history. Its widespread use means that generations of pilots have learned the same basic skills in some version of the Skyhawk.

It also demonstrates how aircraft technology can evolve while the basic airframe remains recognizable.

Across different generations of the Cessna 172, a student can study the transition from:

- Mechanical instruments to digital displays
- Venturi-powered gyroscopes to vacuum pumps
- Vacuum-powered instruments to electronic attitude systems
- Carbureted engines to fuel-injected engines
- Basic navigation radios to integrated GPS avionics

## Beginner Questions

### Are “Cessna 172” and “Skyhawk” the same thing?

Skyhawk is the name Cessna uses for many versions of the 172. Pilots commonly use both names when discussing the airplane.

### Why are the wings above the cabin?

The high-wing design provides good downward visibility and allows gravity to assist with fuel flow from the wing tanks.

### Why is it used for flight training?

It has stable handling, relatively low landing speeds, straightforward controls, and room for an instructor to sit beside the student.

### Can four adults always fly in it together?

No. Four seats do not automatically mean the airplane can safely carry four adults, full fuel, and baggage at the same time.

The pilot must calculate the aircraft’s total weight and center of gravity before flight.

### Is it a cargo airplane?

No. It has a baggage compartment and can carry light equipment, but it was not designed as a dedicated cargo aircraft.

### Does the Coast Guard fly Cessna 172s?

The Cessna 172 is not a standard active-duty Coast Guard aircraft. However, privately owned airplanes, including suitable Cessna models, may be inspected and approved for Coast Guard Auxiliary missions.

### What is the horn-shaped object on some older airplanes?

It is probably a venturi tube. Air passing through it creates suction that can power gyroscopic instruments. It is not an air horn and does not make a warning sound.

### Do all Cessna 172s have glass cockpits?

No. Current-production Skyhawks have Garmin glass cockpits, but many older aircraft still use traditional round instruments. Some older airplanes have been upgraded with newer avionics.

## Vocabulary

**Aileron:** A movable surface on the wing that controls roll.

**Avionics:** Electronic equipment used for communication, navigation, flight information, and aircraft monitoring.

**Center of gravity:** The point where an aircraft’s weight is considered to be concentrated.

**Fixed-pitch propeller:** A propeller whose blade angle cannot be changed during flight.

**Flaps:** Movable wing surfaces used to increase lift and drag, especially during takeoff and landing.

**Glass cockpit:** An instrument panel that displays flight information on electronic screens.

**High-wing aircraft:** An airplane with its wings mounted above the cabin or fuselage.

**KCAS:** Knots calibrated airspeed.

**KTAS:** Knots true airspeed.

**Pitot tube:** A forward-facing tube that measures air pressure for the airspeed indicator.

**Piston engine:** An engine that uses moving pistons to turn a crankshaft and propeller.

**Service ceiling:** The altitude at which an aircraft’s ability to continue climbing becomes limited to a specified rate.

**Static port:** An opening that measures the atmospheric pressure surrounding the aircraft.

**Tricycle landing gear:** Landing gear consisting of two main wheels and a nose wheel.

**Useful load:** The combined allowable weight of fuel, occupants, baggage, and other removable equipment.

**Vacuum pump:** A pump that creates suction to operate certain traditional gyroscopic instruments.

**Venturi tube:** A narrowed tube that creates lower pressure as air passes through it.

**Weight and balance:** The calculation used to confirm that an aircraft’s total weight and center of gravity are within approved limits.
