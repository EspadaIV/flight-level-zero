## What Is It?

The Ford 4-AT Trimotor was an American passenger and cargo airplane developed during the 1920s.

It had three piston engines, a high wing, fixed landing gear, and a primarily aluminum structure. Its corrugated metal skin gave it the nickname **Tin Goose**.

The letters **AT** stood for **Air Transport**. The 4-AT was the first successful production version of Ford’s Trimotor design and preceded the larger Ford 5-AT.

The first 4-AT flew on June 11, 1926. Ford continued building 4-AT variants into the early 1930s.

## What Was It Designed to Do?

The Ford 4-AT was designed to make passenger and cargo transportation by air more practical and dependable.

It was used for:

- Scheduled airline service
- Passenger transportation
- Air mail
- Cargo and freight
- Corporate transportation
- Advertising
- Aerial exploration
- Military transportation
- Crop dusting
- Aerial firefighting
- Smokejumper operations
- Sightseeing flights

The passenger seats could be removed so the cabin could carry cargo.

## Major Features

- Three air-cooled radial engines
- High-mounted wing
- Corrugated aluminum skin
- Enclosed passenger cabin
- Dual pilot controls
- Fixed conventional landing gear
- Tailwheel
- Thick wing that contributed to stable flight
- Removable passenger seats
- Large cabin for its time
- External engine nacelles beneath the wings
- No pressurization
- No retractable landing gear
- Primarily mechanical flight controls and instruments

The earliest Trimotors had an open cockpit for the pilots. Later production aircraft enclosed the cockpit to protect the flight crew from weather.

## 4-AT Variants

### 4-AT-A

The 4-AT-A was an early production version. It used three 200-horsepower Wright Whirlwind J-4 radial engines.

Early cabin arrangements generally carried approximately eight passengers, although capacity varied by configuration.

### 4-AT-B

The 4-AT-B used more powerful Wright J-5 Whirlwind engines and could carry more passengers than earlier versions.

Richard Byrd’s South Pole aircraft, *Floyd Bennett*, was a modified Ford 4-AT-B.

### 4-AT-E

The 4-AT-E was a later and more powerful version. It used three Wright J-6-9 Whirlwind radial engines producing approximately 300 horsepower each.

Its greater power increased its maximum speed, rate of climb, service ceiling, and allowable weight.

## How It Flies

The Ford 4-AT was designed for stability rather than speed or maneuverability.

Its large, thick wing generated substantial lift and helped make the aircraft stable in flight. This was useful for carrying passengers, many of whom had never flown before.

The airplane was slow by modern airline standards. Its corrugated metal skin, fixed landing gear, exposed engine cylinders, struts, and other external structures created considerable aerodynamic drag.

The pilot had to manage three separate engines. If one engine failed, the remaining engines could continue producing power, but the unequal thrust could pull the aircraft toward the failed engine.

Historical descriptions claimed that the Trimotor could fly well with two engines and maintain level flight with one. Actual single-engine performance depended on aircraft weight, altitude, temperature, which engine failed, and the condition of the remaining engines.

## Systems Worth Knowing

### Radial Engines

The Ford 4-AT used three air-cooled radial engines.

In a radial engine, the cylinders are arranged in a circle around the crankshaft. Air flowing over the exposed cylinders removes heat from the engine.

The 4-AT’s engine installation included:

- One engine in the airplane’s nose
- One engine beneath the left wing
- One engine beneath the right wing

Different 4-AT variants used different Wright Whirlwind engines.

### Multi-Engine Control

Each engine required its own controls and monitoring.

The pilots had to manage:

- Engine power
- Engine speed
- Fuel supply
- Oil pressure
- Oil temperature
- Engine synchronization
- Propeller behavior

Operating three engines created a much larger workload than operating a single-engine airplane.

### Asymmetric Thrust

If one engine failed, the operating engines continued pushing the aircraft forward while the failed engine produced little or no thrust.

This uneven force is called **asymmetric thrust**. It causes the airplane to yaw toward the failed engine.

The pilot must use the rudder and other controls to maintain directional control.

### Airframe

The Ford 4-AT used corrugated aluminum skin over a metal structure.

The corrugations increased the stiffness of the metal panels. This allowed the aircraft to use relatively thin sheets of aluminum without requiring a completely smooth external surface.

The design was rugged, but the corrugated surface also created more aerodynamic drag than the smooth skin used on later airliners.

### Flight Controls

The cockpit had dual flight controls so either pilot could control the aircraft.

The controls were mechanical. The pilots moved the aircraft’s control surfaces through cables, pulleys, and linkages rather than hydraulic or electronic systems.

There were no computers to correct the pilot’s inputs.

### Landing Gear

The 4-AT used fixed conventional landing gear.

It had:

- Two main landing wheels
- A small wheel beneath the tail
- No mechanism for retracting the landing gear

Fixed landing gear was simpler and more dependable, but it created drag during flight.

### Instruments

The cockpit contained mechanical flight and engine instruments.

Depending on the aircraft and period, the pilots used instruments showing:

- Airspeed
- Altitude
- Direction
- Rate of climb or descent
- Engine speed
- Oil pressure
- Oil temperature
- Fuel information

It did not have GPS, digital displays, weather radar, or a modern autopilot.

### Cabin

The passenger cabin was enclosed, but it was not pressurized.

Passengers experienced:

- Considerable engine noise
- Vibration
- Changes in temperature
- Lower cabin comfort than a modern airliner
- Direct exposure to changes in altitude and atmospheric pressure

The enclosed cabin was nevertheless a major improvement over the open-cockpit aircraft many people associated with early aviation.

## Why It Matters

The Ford 4-AT helped establish public confidence in commercial air travel.

During the 1920s, many Americans associated airplanes with military accidents, barnstorming, and dangerous experimental flights. Ford’s reputation for dependable automobiles helped make the idea of a Ford-built passenger airplane feel more trustworthy.

The 4-AT also demonstrated several important aviation developments:

- All-metal transport aircraft
- Scheduled passenger service
- Multi-engine redundancy
- Enclosed passenger cabins
- Airline cargo operations
- Dual flight controls
- Industrial aircraft production
- Conversion of one airframe for several different missions

Ford attempted to apply automobile-style production methods to airplane manufacturing. The company built Trimotors in larger numbers than many competing aircraft of the period.

## The South Pole Flight

Explorer Richard E. Byrd selected a Ford 4-AT-B for his Antarctic expedition.

The aircraft was named *Floyd Bennett* in honor of Byrd’s friend and former pilot, who had died in 1928.

On November 28 and 29, 1929, Byrd and a crew of three used the aircraft to complete the first flight over the South Pole.

The expedition’s 4-AT-B was modified to carry the equipment and fuel needed for polar operations. It should not be treated as identical to a normal passenger-configured 4-AT-B.

The aircraft is now preserved at The Henry Ford Museum of American Innovation in Dearborn, Michigan.

## Beginner Questions

### Did the Ford automobile company really build airplanes?

Yes. Ford purchased the Stout Metal Airplane Company in 1925 and produced the Trimotor through its aircraft division.

### Why did it have three engines?

Aircraft engines of the 1920s were less reliable than modern engines. Three engines provided additional power and allowed the aircraft to continue flying after some engine failures.

### What does 4-AT mean?

The letters **AT** mean **Air Transport**. The number identifies the design series.

It does not mean the airplane had four engines. The 4-AT had three.

### What was the Tin Goose?

Tin Goose was the Ford Trimotor’s nickname. It referred to the airplane’s silver-colored corrugated metal skin.

### What is the difference between the 4-AT and 5-AT?

The 5-AT was a larger and more powerful development of the 4-AT. It had a longer wingspan, more cabin space, and greater passenger capacity.

### How many passengers could the 4-AT carry?

Passenger capacity depended on the subvariant and cabin arrangement. Early versions generally carried about eight passengers. Later 4-ATs could carry approximately eleven or twelve.

### Was it comfortable?

It was considered advanced for the 1920s because it had an enclosed cabin, but it was noisy, vibrated heavily, and lacked modern heating, soundproofing, and pressurization.

### Could it fly with only one engine?

Historical accounts state that a lightly loaded Trimotor could maintain level flight on one engine under favorable conditions.

That should not be interpreted as a guarantee. Performance depended on weight, altitude, weather, aircraft condition, and which engines remained operational.

### Was it faster than a modern car?

Its cruise speed was roughly 95 to 107 mph, depending on the version. That was fast compared with most ground transportation in the 1920s but extremely slow compared with a modern airliner.

### Are any Ford 4-ATs still flying?

A small number of restored 4-AT aircraft remain airworthy. Their engines and systems may differ from their original factory configurations.

## Vocabulary

**Air-cooled engine:** An engine cooled by air flowing around its cylinders.

**Air transport:** The movement of passengers, mail, or cargo by aircraft.

**Asymmetric thrust:** Unequal thrust caused when engines on different parts of an aircraft produce different amounts of power.

**Conventional landing gear:** Landing gear with two main wheels and a tailwheel.

**Corrugated metal:** Metal formed into repeating ridges to increase stiffness.

**Drag:** Aerodynamic resistance that opposes an aircraft’s movement through the air.

**Engine nacelle:** A housing or structure that holds an aircraft engine.

**Fixed landing gear:** Landing gear that remains outside the aircraft during flight.

**Monoplane:** An aircraft with one primary set of wings.

**Multi-engine aircraft:** An aircraft powered by more than one engine.

**Radial engine:** A piston engine with cylinders arranged around a central crankshaft.

**Redundancy:** The use of additional components so a system can continue operating after one component fails.

**Service ceiling:** The altitude where an aircraft’s ability to continue climbing becomes limited to a specified rate.

**Tailwheel:** A small wheel located beneath the tail of an aircraft.

**Unpressurized cabin:** A cabin that remains at approximately the same atmospheric pressure as the surrounding air.

**Yaw:** Rotation of an aircraft’s nose to the left or right.
